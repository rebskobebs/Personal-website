Labor & Migration Completed · 2023

Nepali Migrant Workers
in the Gulf

Research into the working and living conditions of Nepali labor migrants in Gulf Cooperation Council states — examining the kafala system, recruitment debt traps, welfare gaps, and the diplomatic failures that leave workers without meaningful protection.

Scale of the Issue

Nepal sends more labor migrants per capita than almost any other country in the world. Approximately 1.3 million Nepalis are employed across GCC nations at any given time — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, and Bahrain — primarily in construction, domestic work, and low-skilled services. This migration is not a marginal phenomenon: it is a structural feature of Nepal's economy, enabling remittances that constitute over 26% of GDP and sustain millions of households.

Yet the conditions under which this migration occurs — and the conditions in which migrants live and work — have been a source of consistent international concern. Death rates among Nepali workers in GCC states remain high, wage theft is endemic, and workers frequently arrive in debt from recruitment fees that can exceed a year's earnings.

The Kafala System

The kafala (sponsorship) system ties a migrant worker's legal status directly to their employer. In practice, this means workers cannot change employers, leave the country, or seek legal redress without their sponsor's consent — a structural condition that creates near-total employer power and near-total worker vulnerability.

While several GCC states have announced reforms — Qatar's abolition of the exit permit requirement in 2020 being the most prominent — implementation has been uneven and the underlying power asymmetry of the sponsorship relationship remains largely intact. This research examines both the structure of kafala and the gap between reform announcements and on-the-ground reality.

Recruitment Debt

One of the most persistent and damaging features of Nepali labor migration is the recruitment fee system. Workers pay brokers — in Nepal and in destination countries — fees that can reach NPR 100,000–300,000 (USD 750–2,200) for jobs that may pay NPR 15,000–30,000 per month. Workers frequently borrow at high interest to pay these fees, beginning their migration already in debt. This debt structure functions as a form of bonded labor: workers cannot leave jobs or assert rights without losing the income needed to repay what they owe.

Diplomatic Failures

Nepal has bilateral labor agreements with several GCC states, but enforcement is weak and Nepal's diplomatic leverage in these relationships is limited. The research examines why destination-country reform has been so slow and why Nepal's own foreign policy has struggled to secure protections for its workers — arguing that the political economy of migration governance in Nepal systematically underweights migrant welfare relative to remittance flows and bilateral relationships.

Policy Implications

The research identifies three domains where reform is both urgent and feasible: fee regulation and enforcement in Nepal's recruitment sector; strengthened labor attaché capacity in GCC embassies; and bilateral agreements with meaningful monitoring and enforcement mechanisms rather than declaratory commitments. It also argues that civil society and diaspora organizations in GCC states play an underappreciated role in worker protection that policy should support.
